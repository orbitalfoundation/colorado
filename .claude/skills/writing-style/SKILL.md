# Writing

Revised 12 November 2026

Writing guidance lives in this file. Other files may point here, and none adds
rules of its own. The assignment sets the audience, purpose and form.

## Aim

Make the meaning easy to follow and give the reader a reason to keep
following. Sound like a knowledgeable, engaged person explaining the work to
an intelligent reader who was not there when it was done. Be precise and
readable, and be willing to sound ordinary.

The interest comes from what is consequential, surprising or unresolved in the
material. Do not manufacture significance the material does not support.

## Before drafting

- **The reader.** Unless told otherwise: intelligent, interested, and has not
  seen the code, the conversation or the devlog. A label coined during the
  work means nothing to them.
- **The facts.** Establish which numbers and descriptions are current. Where
  sources disagree in a way that affects the account, report it. Do not smooth
  it over. Ask only when the conflict blocks a responsible draft.
- **The form.** A project page gives one main account that reads straight
  through, with methods, provenance and history in supporting sections or on
  linked pages. A qualification that changes how a claim should be read stays
  beside the claim.
- **The order.** Follow the reader's questions, usually: what is this, why
  does it matter, what was done, what was learned, what remains uncertain.
  They are a starting point, not headings.

## Holding the reader's attention

Explain why things happen and what changes as a result. A run of facts does
not hold a reader. A consequence, an unexpected result, an obstacle or an
unresolved dependency does. Give an important idea room, with an example or a
labelled hypothetical where it helps. This one makes a dependency visible:

> Suppose a hospital survives the shaking and switches to its backup
> generator. For now, it has power. Its next fuel delivery is less certain:
> the tanker is across the river, and the bridge is closed. To estimate how
> long the hospital can operate, the simulation needs to represent fuel
> deliveries as well as the fuel already in its tanks.

Learn from how it works. Do not reuse its phrasing, structure or subject. A
paragraph may also simply establish facts, with no surprise and no resonant
last line.

## Words and sentences

- Use the ordinary expression, including the ordinary professional one. "The
  fuel the fixing runs on" is short words the reader still has to translate.
  "The fuel needed for emergency response and repairs" needs no translation.
- Say what a thing is before giving it a nickname. Expand an acronym once.
- A sentence follows one line of thought. A claim and its qualification belong
  together. Split a sentence whose clauses compete: one that sets the scene,
  fixes a date, names a source and adds a caveat is doing four jobs.
- Do not compress. If the reader has to reconstruct how two ideas relate, the
  sentence is too short. Fragments suit headings, labels, captions and slides.
- When the trouble is where something sits, move it. Rewording will not help.
- Describe a thing, not its position. "The first venture", "the latest",
  "today", a box name or a relative path is a fact about the session, and it
  rots when a folder moves or a second thing arrives. Paths go in config that
  code reads, not in prose. Dated devlog entries may say "first" and "today".
- No em-dashes. Anselm does not use them. Use a comma, a colon, parentheses or
  two sentences, and not a chain of semicolons.

## Evidence and claims

- Show which level a sentence is about: the real world, the chosen scenario,
  the model, or one run. Write "the model does not represent hospital bed
  capacity", not "the hospitals have no beds". Do this where confusion would
  matter, not as a formula on every sentence.
- Write what a source states. Where there is no source, say the thing is
  unknown, or say it is a guess. Never present a remembered detail as
  verified. Never invent a person, event, quotation or experience and present
  it as real.
- State a limit concretely: what is unverified, what is missing, and what that
  stops a reader from concluding. Do not call the work honest, rigorous or
  careful. The disclosure does that job.
- "Nobody has done this", "the best available" and "the field agrees" need
  their evidence in view.
- Credit the originator of a borrowed term or idea. Cite quotations and
  findings.
- The conclusion matches what was established: a finding, a qualified
  judgement, an open question or a next step. Do not tell the reader how to
  feel.

## Revising

Draft for the reader first. Then reread as that reader and fix every place
where they would have to guess what a word refers to, unpack a compressed
phrase, or accept a conclusion wider than the evidence. Check every name,
number and attribution against its source. When updating a document, remove or
reconcile what the change supersedes, everywhere it appears. Do not append a
second account of the same thing.

Then read for rhythm. These habits recur in machine drafts:

- a contrast forced where there is none ("not X, but Y");
- a closing line added only to land a point;
- a sentence that grades what comes next ("the honest answer is");
- a list that swells into a clause at the end ("grids, generation, demand and
  the networks that carry it");
- stacked hedges;
- the same cadence in sentence after sentence.

They are symptoms to notice, not bans. A contrast or an emphatic sentence that
does real work stays. If avoiding a habit produces a stranger sentence, undo
the edit. Keep the best lines.

## Writing for Anselm

- Essays under his name are his to write. Help with structure, evidence,
  observed particulars and alternatives. Mark drafted prose as machine-drafted
  and expect him to rewrite it.
- When editing his prose, keep his phrasing, edges and specifics. A tidier
  version that loses them is worse.
- His essay ["How to write less bad"](https://anselm.substack.com/p/how-to-write-less-bad)
  shows his taste. It is background, not a checklist or a voice to imitate.
- Leave him out of public copy unless a public source documents his
  contribution or he led the work.
- Working documents, such as devlog entries or a packet for an accountant,
  are brief: the decision or request first, one line for a settled item.
  Progress notes and promises of future work belong there, not on a public
  page.
- For advocacy and opinion, his stance: more environmentalist than humanist;
  systems solutions that help people across political divisions; "maximize
  diversity" and "keeping futures open" over minimising harm; no political or
  sexual positioning that makes readers take sides without serving the piece.
  This governs framing, never what the evidence supports.
